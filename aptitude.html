1 Percentages +

Notes

  • A percentage is just a fraction expressed out of 100 — 25% is the same as 25/100 or 0.25.
  • "x% of y" always equals "y% of x" — pick whichever is easier to compute mentally.
  • To find what percentage A is of B, compute (A / B) × 100.

Formulas

Percentage change = (New − Old) / Old × 100 Successive % change = a + b + (a × b / 100) New value after change = Old × (1 ± rate/100)

Practice Questions

  1. A number is increased by 20% and then decreased by 20%. What is the net percentage change?

    Answer: A net decrease of 4%. Using successive change: 20 − 20 + (20 × −20)/100 = −4%.

  2. In an election, a candidate got 65% of the votes and won by 4,200 votes. What was the total number of votes cast?

    Answer: 14,000. Winning margin = 30% of total = 4,200, so total = 4,200 / 0.30 = 14,000.

2 Profit & Loss +

Notes

  • Cost Price (CP) is what the seller paid; Selling Price (SP) is what the buyer pays.
  • Marked Price (MP) is the "sticker price" before any discount is applied.
  • When two articles are sold at the same price with equal % profit and loss, there is always a net loss.

Formulas

Profit % = (SP − CP) / CP × 100 Loss % = (CP − SP) / CP × 100 SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100)

Practice Questions

  1. A shopkeeper marks an item 40% above cost price and gives a 25% discount. What is his overall profit or loss %?

    Answer: 5% profit. Take CP = 100 → MP = 140 → SP = 140 × 0.75 = 105, so profit = 5%.

  2. By selling an article for ₹720, a man loses 10%. At what price should he sell it to gain 15%?

    Answer: ₹920. CP = 720 / 0.90 = ₹800. Required SP = 800 × 1.15 = ₹920.

3 Time, Speed & Distance +

Notes

  • Speed, distance and time always follow the same core relationship — fix one to solve for the other two.
  • For a moving train crossing a platform, the distance covered = length of train + length of platform.
  • In boats and streams, "downstream" adds the current's speed, "upstream" subtracts it.

Formulas

Speed = Distance / Time km/h to m/s: multiply by 5/18 Downstream speed = boat + stream, Upstream speed = boat − stream

Practice Questions

  1. A train 150 m long crosses a platform 250 m long in 20 seconds. What is the speed of the train in km/h?

    Answer: 72 km/h. Total distance = 400 m in 20 s → 20 m/s → 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h.

  2. A boat covers 32 km downstream in 4 hours and the same distance upstream in 8 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water.

    Answer: 6 km/h. Downstream speed = 8 km/h, upstream speed = 4 km/h. Boat speed = (8 + 4)/2 = 6 km/h.

4 Time & Work +

Notes

  • Think in terms of "work done per day" (1/n of the job) rather than tracking the whole job at once.
  • When people work together, add their individual one-day work rates.
  • Efficiency and time taken are inversely proportional — more efficient means less time.

Formulas

If A finishes work in n days, A's 1-day work = 1/n Combined 1-day work = 1/A + 1/B Total time together = 1 / (1/A + 1/B)

Practice Questions

  1. A can do a piece of work in 12 days and B in 18 days. In how many days can they finish it together?

    Answer: 7.2 days. Combined rate = 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36, so time = 36/5 = 7.2 days.

  2. A and B together can finish a job in 10 days. A alone can do it in 15 days. How long would B alone take?

    Answer: 30 days. B's rate = 1/10 − 1/15 = 1/30, so B alone takes 30 days.

5 Simple & Compound Interest +

Notes

  • Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, every year.
  • Compound interest is calculated on principal + accumulated interest, so it grows faster.
  • For short durations (1–2 years) at the same rate, CI is only slightly more than SI.

Formulas

SI = (P × R × T) / 100 CI = P × (1 + R/100)^T − P Difference between CI and SI for 2 years = P × (R/100)²

Practice Questions

  1. Find the compound interest on ₹10,000 for 2 years at 10% per annum, compounded annually.

    Answer: ₹2,100. Amount = 10,000 × 1.1² = 12,100, so CI = ₹2,100.

  2. The difference between CI and SI on a sum for 2 years at 5% per annum is ₹25. Find the sum.

    Answer: ₹10,000. Using difference = P × (R/100)², 25 = P × 0.0025, so P = ₹10,000.

6 HCF & LCM +

Notes

  • HCF (Highest Common Factor) is the largest number that divides two or more numbers exactly.
  • LCM (Lowest Common Multiple) is the smallest number that is divisible by all the given numbers.
  • For fractions, find HCF using numerators/denominators separately rather than converting to decimals.

Formulas

HCF × LCM = Product of the two numbers (for exactly two numbers) HCF of fractions = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators) LCM of fractions = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators)

Practice Questions

  1. Find the HCF and LCM of 24 and 36.

    Answer: HCF = 12, LCM = 72. Since HCF × LCM = 24 × 36 = 864, LCM = 864 / 12 = 72.

  2. The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 240. If one number is 48, find the other.

    Answer: 60. Product of numbers = HCF × LCM = 12 × 240 = 2,880. Other number = 2,880 / 48 = 60.

7 Permutation & Combination +

Notes

  • Permutation counts arrangements where order matters; combination counts selections where order doesn't.
  • Use permutation for "arrangement", "ranking", or "seating" problems, and combination for "selection" or "grouping" problems.
  • Factorial notation n! means n × (n−1) × (n−2) × ... × 1, with 0! defined as 1.

Formulas

nPr = n! / (n − r)! nCr = n! / (r! × (n − r)!) nCr = nC(n − r)

Practice Questions

  1. In how many ways can 5 people be seated in a row?

    Answer: 120 ways. 5 people in 5 seats = 5! = 120.

  2. From a group of 7 people, in how many ways can a committee of 3 be formed?

    Answer: 35 ways. 7C3 = 7! / (3! × 4!) = 35.

8 Probability +

Notes

  • Probability of an event = favourable outcomes / total possible outcomes, and always lies between 0 and 1.
  • For independent events, multiply individual probabilities to get the probability of both happening.
  • For mutually exclusive events, add individual probabilities to get the probability of either happening.

Formulas

P(Event) = Favourable outcomes / Total outcomes P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), for independent events P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), for mutually exclusive events

Practice Questions

  1. A die is rolled once. What is the probability of getting a number greater than 4?

    Answer: 1/3. Favourable outcomes {5, 6} = 2, total outcomes = 6, so probability = 2/6 = 1/3.

  2. A bag contains 4 red and 6 blue balls. One ball is drawn at random. What is the probability it is red?

    Answer: 2/5. Favourable = 4, total = 10, so probability = 4/10 = 2/5.

9 Number System +

Notes

  • Natural numbers start from 1; whole numbers include 0; integers additionally include negative numbers.
  • A number is divisible by 3 if its digit sum is divisible by 3, and by 9 if its digit sum is divisible by 9.
  • Prime numbers have exactly two factors (1 and itself); 2 is the only even prime number.

Formulas

Sum of first n natural numbers = n(n + 1) / 2 Sum of first n squares = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) / 6 Divisibility by 11: (sum of digits at odd places) − (sum of digits at even places) is 0 or a multiple of 11

Practice Questions

  1. What is the remainder when 2^50 is divided by 7?

    Answer: 4. Since 2^3 = 8 ≡ 1 (mod 7), 2^50 = (2^3)^16 × 2^2 ≡ 1 × 4 = 4 (mod 7).

  2. Find the sum of the first 20 natural numbers.

    Answer: 210. Using n(n + 1)/2 = (20 × 21)/2 = 210.

10 Averages +

Notes

  • Average is the sum of all values divided by the number of values.
  • Adding a new value shifts the average toward that value, proportional to the size of the group.
  • Weighted average is used when items in a group carry different importance or quantity.

Formulas

Average = Sum of observations / Number of observations New average after adding one term = (Old sum + New value) / (n + 1) Weighted average = (w1x1 + w2x2 + ...) / (w1 + w2 + ...)

Practice Questions

  1. The average of 5 numbers is 20. If one number is excluded, the average becomes 19. Find the excluded number.

    Answer: 24. Original sum = 5 × 20 = 100; remaining sum = 4 × 19 = 76; excluded number = 100 − 76 = 24.

  2. The average age of a class of 30 students is 15 years. Including the teacher's age, the average becomes 15.5. Find the teacher's age.

    Answer: 30.5 years. Original total = 30 × 15 = 450; new total = 31 × 15.5 = 480.5; teacher's age = 480.5 − 450 = 30.5.

Less about formulas, more about pattern spotting — the fastest way to improve here is timed practice.

1 Blood Relations +

Notes

  • Draw a quick family tree as you read — don't try to hold the relationships in your head.
  • Use "+" for male and "−" for female next to each name to avoid confusion later in the question.
  • Watch for indirect phrasing like "the only son of my mother's mother" — that's simply "my uncle/maternal uncle".

Key Rules

Mother's/Father's son = Brother Son's wife = Daughter-in-law Father's/Mother's father = Grandfather

Practice Questions

  1. Pointing to a photograph, a man says, "She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son." How is the girl related to the man?

    Answer: Sister. "My grandfather's only son" is the man's father, so the girl is his father's daughter — his sister.

  2. A is B's sister. C is B's mother. D is C's father. E is D's mother. How is A related to D?

    Answer: Granddaughter. C is D's daughter, and A is C's daughter, so A is D's granddaughter.

2 Coding–Decoding +

Notes

  • Write out the alphabet with position numbers (A=1 … Z=26) on rough paper before you start.
  • Check if the code shifts letters by a fixed number, reverses the word, or substitutes by a pattern.
  • Once you crack the rule on the example given, apply the exact same rule to the actual question.

Key Rules

Forward shift by n: letter position + n Reverse coding: A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X ...

Practice Questions

  1. In a code, "CAT" is written as "DBU". How is "DOG" written in the same code?

    Answer: EPH. Each letter is shifted forward by 1: D→E, O→P, G→H.

  2. If "PAPER" is coded as "QCSIW", what is the coding pattern applied to each letter?

    Answer: Each letter shifts forward by an increasing amount (+1, +2, +3, +4, +5): P→Q, A→C, P→S, E→I, R→W.

3 Series & Sequences +

Notes

  • Check the difference between consecutive terms first — constant, increasing, or alternating.
  • If differences don't reveal a pattern, try ratios, squares/cubes, or alternate-term series.
  • For letter series, convert to numbers (A=1...Z=26) to spot the pattern faster.

Key Rules

Arithmetic series: constant difference between terms Geometric series: constant ratio between terms

Practice Questions

  1. Find the next term: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?

    Answer: 42. Differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 — each term is n(n+1), so the next is 6×7 = 42.

  2. Find the missing letter: B, D, G, K, ?

    Answer: P. The gap between letters increases by 1 each time (+2, +3, +4, +5): K + 5 = P.

4 Syllogisms +

Notes

  • Draw Venn diagrams for each statement rather than reasoning in your head.
  • Evaluate a conclusion as true only if it holds in every possible diagram, not just one.
  • "Some" statements only guarantee overlap — they don't say anything about the rest of the group.

Key Rules

All A are B + All B are C → All A are C Some A are B does NOT imply Some B are A is false — it's actually always true (reversible)

Practice Questions

  1. Statements: All pens are books. All books are tables. Conclusion: All pens are tables. Is the conclusion valid?

    Answer: Valid. This is a direct chain: All A are B, All B are C, so All A are C holds in every possible diagram.

  2. Statements: Some cats are dogs. Some dogs are rats. Conclusion: Some cats are rats. Is the conclusion valid?

    Answer: Not valid. Two "some" statements never combine into a guaranteed conclusion — the overlaps might not intersect.

5 Puzzles & Seating Arrangement +

Notes

  • Read all clues once fully before drawing anything, then place the most specific clue first.
  • For circular arrangements, fix one person's position to stop the whole diagram from "rotating" in your head.
  • Cross off clues as you use them so you don't accidentally reapply or skip one.

Key Rules

"Facing centre" in a circle: left/right are as the person sees them "Facing outward": left/right are reversed from an outside view

Practice Questions

  1. Five friends P, Q, R, S, T sit in a row. Q is immediately right of P. R is at one end. S is between Q and T. Who is at the other end?

    Answer: One valid order is R, P, Q, S, T — so T sits at the other end, opposite R.

  2. Six people sit around a circular table facing the centre. A is second to the right of B, and C is immediately left of A. Where is C relative to B?

    Answer: C is immediately to the right of B — since A is two seats right of B and C sits just before A (going clockwise), C lands directly next to B.

6 Number Series +

Notes

  • Check the difference or ratio between consecutive numbers before guessing the next term.
  • Some series mix two interleaved patterns — check the odd-position and even-position terms separately.
  • Squares, cubes, and prime-number series are common; recognise the first 15–20 of each by memory.

Key Rules

Check simple difference/ratio pattern first If no pattern is found, split into two alternating sub-series Try squares (1, 4, 9, 16...), cubes (1, 8, 27, 64...), or primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11...)

Practice Questions

  1. Find the next term: 5, 11, 23, 47, ?

    Answer: 95. Each term follows ×2 + 1: 5×2+1=11, 11×2+1=23, 23×2+1=47, 47×2+1=95.

  2. Find the missing term: 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, ?

    Answer: 48. Differences are 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 (increasing by 2), so 35 + 13 = 48.

7 Direction Sense +

Notes

  • Sketch a rough compass (N, S, E, W) and mark the starting point before plotting any movements.
  • A left turn rotates you 90° anticlockwise; a right turn rotates you 90° clockwise from your current facing direction.
  • To find the shortest distance between the start and end points, apply the Pythagorean theorem to the net horizontal and vertical displacement.

Key Rules

Facing North, left turn → West; right turn → East Net displacement: distance = √(x² + y²) Facing the sunrise means facing East; facing the sunset means facing West

Practice Questions

  1. A man walks 4 km North, then turns right and walks 3 km. How far is he from his starting point?

    Answer: 5 km. He now stands 4 km North and 3 km East of start — straight-line distance = √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5 km.

  2. Ravi walks 6 km East, turns left and walks 6 km, then turns left again and walks 6 km. In which direction is he from his starting point?

    Answer: North. His path forms three sides of a square — East, then North, then West — cancelling the East move and leaving him 6 km due North of the start.

8 Calendar & Clock +

Notes

  • An ordinary year has 365 days (52 weeks + 1 odd day); a leap year has 366 days (52 weeks + 2 odd days).
  • A calendar repeats for a given year when the total odd days between the two years is 0 and February has the same number of days.
  • On a clock, the minute hand moves 6° per minute and the hour hand moves 0.5° per minute — use their relative speed for angle problems.

Key Rules

Angle between hands = |(11/2 × M) − (30 × H)| degrees Odd days: ordinary year = 1, leap year = 2 A correct clock completes a 12-hour cycle in 720 minutes

Practice Questions

  1. If 1st January 2026 is a Thursday, what day of the week is 1st January 2027?

    Answer: Friday. 2026 is not a leap year, so it has 1 odd day — the day advances by exactly one, from Thursday to Friday.

  2. At what time between 3 and 4 o'clock will the hands of a clock first be at right angles?

    Answer: About 3:32:44. Solving (11/2)M − 90 = 90 gives M ≈ 32.7 minutes past 3.

Vocabulary and grammar reward daily practice more than last-minute cramming — little and often works best here.

1 Reading Comprehension +

Notes

  • Skim the passage once for the main idea before reading the questions — don't hunt line by line first.
  • Answers to fact-based questions are usually stated directly; don't overthink them.
  • For "tone" or "inference" questions, look for the author's word choice rather than your own opinion.

Key Rules

Main idea = usually in the first or last sentence of a paragraph Eliminate extreme-word options ("always", "never") unless the passage itself is extreme

Practice Questions

  1. Passage: "Remote work reduced commute times but blurred the boundary between office hours and personal time for many employees." What is the passage's main point?

    Answer: Remote work brought a trade-off — it saved commute time but made work-life boundaries less clear.

  2. In the same passage, what tone does the author take toward remote work?

    Answer: Balanced/neutral — the sentence presents one benefit and one drawback without favoring either.

2 Synonyms & Antonyms +

Notes

  • Learn words in groups by theme (e.g. words for "criticize": rebuke, admonish, reprimand) — it sticks better than isolated lists.
  • If unsure of the exact meaning, judge whether the word feels positive, negative, or neutral and match that tone.
  • Watch for options that are related but not actually synonymous — a common trap in MCQs.

Key Rules

Synonym = closest same meaning, not just "related topic" Antonym questions: eliminate any option that is itself a synonym first

Practice Questions

  1. Choose the synonym of "TRANSIENT": (a) Permanent (b) Fleeting (c) Solid (d) Massive

    Answer: (b) Fleeting — both mean lasting only a short time.

  2. Choose the antonym of "FRUGAL": (a) Thrifty (b) Wasteful (c) Careful (d) Modest

    Answer: (b) Wasteful — frugal means careful with money/resources, so its opposite is wasteful.

3 Sentence Correction +

Notes

  • Check subject-verb agreement first — it's the most commonly tested error.
  • Read the sentence in your head with each option substituted; the correct one should sound natural, not just "less wrong".
  • Keep tense consistent across a sentence unless the meaning clearly requires a shift.

Key Rules

Singular subject → singular verb; Plural subject → plural verb "Neither...nor" and "Either...or" agree with the nearer subject

Practice Questions

  1. Correct the error: "Each of the students have submitted their assignment."

    Answer: "Each of the students has submitted their assignment." — "Each" is singular, so it takes "has", not "have".

  2. Correct the error: "Neither the manager nor the employees was informed about the change."

    Answer: "...were informed..." — the verb agrees with the nearer subject "employees", which is plural.

4 Para Jumbles +

Notes

  • Find the sentence that introduces the topic — it usually doesn't contain pronouns like "it" or "this" referring back.
  • Look for linking words ("however", "therefore", "this") to chain sentences together.
  • Once you have a likely first and last sentence, the middle order is often easier to lock in.

Key Rules

Opening line: introduces subject, no dangling pronoun Closing line: often has a concluding word ("thus", "finally", "in the end")

Practice Questions

  1. Arrange into a coherent paragraph: (1) This made the harvest larger than expected. (2) The monsoon arrived early this year. (3) Farmers were initially worried about the timing. (4) However, the rains were steady and well-spread.

    Answer: 2-3-4-1. Introduce the event (2), reaction (3), contrast with "however" (4), then the result (1).

  2. Which sentence most likely opens the paragraph: "It soon became the city's most visited landmark." / "The old clock tower was restored last year after decades of neglect."

    Answer: "The old clock tower was restored last year after decades of neglect." — it introduces the subject; the other sentence relies on "it" referring back to something already named.

5 One-Word Substitution +

Notes

  • These are best learned in small daily batches of 8–10 words rather than long lists at once.
  • Group words by category (people, places, actions) to make them easier to recall under time pressure.
  • Many of these words also double as vocabulary/synonym questions, so learning them helps both sections.

Key Rules

Learn the word + a one-line definition + a sample sentence together

Practice Questions

  1. One word for "a person who knows a lot about many different subjects": (a) Specialist (b) Polymath (c) Novice (d) Skeptic

    Answer: (b) Polymath.

  2. One word for "a statement that is obviously true and not worth mentioning": (a) Paradox (b) Truism (c) Anomaly (d) Fallacy

    Answer: (b) Truism.

6 Spotting Errors +

Notes

  • Read the sentence in parts (usually split into 3–4 chunks) and check each chunk independently for a grammatical error.
  • Common error zones: subject-verb agreement, article usage (a/an/the), preposition choice, and tense consistency.
  • If no error is found after checking every part, the answer is often "No error" — don't force-fit a mistake.

Key Rules

Check subject-verb agreement, tense, and article usage first — they cover most errors A comparative sentence ("more...than", "as...as") needs matching structure on both sides Certain verbs take fixed prepositions ("depend on", "different from") — memorise the common ones

Practice Questions

  1. Spot the error: "Neither of the two students / have completed / their homework on time."

    Answer: Error in part 2 — "have" should be "has", since "Neither of" takes a singular verb.

  2. Spot the error: "She is enough intelligent / to solve / this problem easily."

    Answer: Error in part 1 — "enough" should follow the adjective: "intelligent enough", not "enough intelligent".

7 Idioms & Phrases +

Notes

  • Learn idioms together with their meaning and a sample sentence, not as a standalone phrase — context helps recall.
  • Most idioms can't be understood literally, so guessing from the individual words rarely works.
  • Group idioms by theme (body parts, animals, colours) to make revision faster and stickier.

Key Rules

An idiom's meaning is figurative, not literal — always learn its intended sense Match the idiom to the closest meaning, not the most literal-sounding option

Practice Questions

  1. Choose the meaning of "to burn the midnight oil": (a) To waste money (b) To work late into the night (c) To start a fire (d) To relax

    Answer: (b) To work late into the night.

  2. Choose the meaning of "to let the cat out of the bag": (a) To adopt a pet (b) To reveal a secret accidentally (c) To escape danger (d) To cause confusion

    Answer: (b) To reveal a secret accidentally.

8 Sentence Completion +

Notes

  • Read the whole sentence first to grasp its overall logic before deciding on the missing word — the second half often signals the answer.
  • Watch for contrast words ("but", "although", "however") and continuation words ("and", "similarly") — they hint whether the blank should match or oppose the rest.
  • For double-blank questions, eliminate any option where even one word doesn't fit before checking the other.

Key Rules

Contrast connectors ("but", "yet", "despite", "although") signal the blank should oppose the rest of the sentence Continuation connectors ("and", "also", "moreover") signal the blank should align with the rest of the sentence

Practice Questions

  1. Complete: "The manager was known for his __________ approach; he always listened carefully before making decisions." (a) Impulsive (b) Thoughtful (c) Careless (d) Hasty

    Answer: (b) Thoughtful — it matches "listened carefully before deciding".

  2. Complete: "Although the weather was __________, the trekking group decided to continue their journey." (a) Pleasant (b) Harsh (c) Sunny (d) Calm

    Answer: (b) Harsh — "Although" signals a contrast: the weather was bad, yet they continued.
